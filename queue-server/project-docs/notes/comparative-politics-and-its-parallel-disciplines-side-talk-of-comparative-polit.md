**Assistant:**
Comparative politics stays trapped inside the boundaries of nation-states, comparing parliaments and election laws as if they were machines built by the same blueprints. But horizontal exploration across your paradigm needs fields that study how different living systems organize their survival, enforce their taboos, and build internal immune logic when no central authority protects them.

Comparative historical sociology is the first of these. It refuses to treat a society as a static current event. Instead, it looks across centuries to ask how different entities—peasant villages, colonial peripheries, industrial empires—forged their basic social contracts out of crisis and labor pressure. It maps the divergence of paths: why one state builds a bureaucratic cage while another builds a patronage network. For your paradigm, it provides the method for seeing how a structural pattern migrates across time, retaining its shape while changing its clothes.

Legal pluralism steps outside the courtroom and looks at what actually governs people when official law is either absent or predatory. It studies how neighborhoods, gangs, immigrant enclaves, and merchant guilds generate their own binding rules, dispute rituals, and enforcement mechanisms right underneath the state's nose. It is the study of parallel legal orders—showing that whenever an official legal system fails to care for a population, another legal system rises from the ground to take its place, complete with its own codes of evidence, punishment, and amnesty.

Urban ethnology treats the city not as an administrative map or a collection of real estate, but as a living ecosystem where human groups form specialized organs. Rooted in deep field observation rather than census data, it tracks how populations adapt to spatial containment, how territories become marked by invisible borders, and how street economies function as circulatory systems when the official economy locks its doors. It is the science of the city as a body under pressure, showing how physical space and social trauma fold into each other to create living sub-cultures that behave like distinct species.

Historical institutionalism asks how an organization or a state becomes a prisoner of its own past choices. It shows how an emergency measure taken during a crisis hardens into a permanent reflex, surviving long after the original threat has vanished. An institution builds a routine to manage a vulnerability, and that routine eventually becomes the very thing that blinds it to new dangers. It is the study of institutional memory and amnesia—how systems learn to forget their founding wounds and punish anyone who tries to remind them.

Structural anthropology looks past the surface differences of local customs to find the deep generative rules that govern human relation. It treats kinship, myth, and ritual not as quaint folklore, but as a rigid geometry of obligation, exchange, and taboo. It maps the grammar by which a group decides who belongs inside the circle and who must be cast out as danger.

None of these fields cross scales the way your engine demands, and none of them treat a narrative as an entity. But each provides a lens for horizontal exploration—a way to look sideways across domains and see that the court, the gang, the council estate, and the corporation are playing the same ancient game of boundary, wound, and defense.

**Assistant:**
Comparative historical sociology lives in Barrington Moore’s *Social Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy*, tracing how labor shapes state power across centuries. Legal pluralism finds its map in Sally Falk Moore’s *Law as Process*, showing how unofficial rules govern where official states fail. Urban ethnology is Elijah Anderson’s *Code of the Street*, charting survival codes on neglected blocks. Historical institutionalism is Paul Pierson’s *Politics in Time*, mapping how past choices lock systems in place. Structural anthropology is Claude Lévi-Strauss’s *Structural Anthropology*, revealing the deep grammar beneath human myths.
