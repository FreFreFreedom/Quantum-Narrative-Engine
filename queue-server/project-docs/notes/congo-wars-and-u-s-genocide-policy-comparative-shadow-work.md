It is a brutal pairing to put side by side, but looking at them through the lens of scaled-up consciousness systems, *Dancing in the Glory of Monsters* and *“A Problem from Hell”* map two different mechanics of institutional and civilizational shadow work—or perhaps, the catastrophic failure to perform it.

Jason Stearns’s tracking of the Congo wars lays bare how macro-structures—nations, militias, corporate resource extraction networks, and regional geopolitics—behave with the unmitigated, predatory drives of a traumatized organism. What happens in the Great Lakes region is a staggering demonstration of self-similarity: the micro-level dynamics of grievance, survival, and unchecked violence scaling up until an entire sub-continent becomes a theater of recursive collapse. The state doesn't just fail; it fractures into autonomous, competing nodes of raw power, mirroring the worst of factional human psychology stripped of any civilizational overlay.

Samantha Power’s *“A Problem from Hell,”* on the other hand, approaches the architecture of atrocity from the vantage point of the observer-state—specifically, the bureaucratic and psychological machinery of American foreign policy. Where Stearns shows the anatomy of the conflagration from within, Power exposes the chilling calculus of *detachment*. She traces how institutions process (or actively suppress) feedback loops of mass violence, turning systemic genocide into a distant, abstract data point managed by risk-averse legalism and political self-preservation. It is a masterclass in how a superpower's collective consciousness constructs blind spots, rationalizing inaction while the structural web of the world unravels.

Placed together, they reveal a terrifying symmetry: the active, ravenous devouring of a region on one end, and the systemic, bureaucratic look-away on the other.
