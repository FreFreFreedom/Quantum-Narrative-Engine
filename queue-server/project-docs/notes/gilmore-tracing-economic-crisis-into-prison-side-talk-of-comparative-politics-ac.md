She shows the exact descent you named: how a geopolitical and economic rearrangement at the top of the state cascades downward into rural towns looking for economic lifeboats, turning human beings into the raw material that absorbs a structural crisis. The book is an unsparing look at how institutions manufacture their own justification by feeding on the communities they have already abandoned.

They provide the raw diagnostic material for what an unbuilt comparative political engine must learn to see: how macro-structures find their final resting place in the human nervous system, and how institutions, blinded by their own specialized mandates, become the primary architects of the very crises they are funded to solve.

**Assistant:**
Forensic ethnography at its most potent is a discipline that follows the living imprint of violence, abandonment, and systemic cruelty—mapping not offices, but wounds, not processes, but what the system actively refuses to feel. In the canon beyond what you’ve already surfaced (*Ghettoside*, *Random Family*, *Death Without Weeping*, *On the Run*, *Crook County*), the genre is rare and hard-earned, but several works stand as living anatomical maps of neglected pain:

**(1) Killing the Black Body: Race, Reproduction, and the Meaning of Liberty — Dorothy Roberts**
Not pure classic ethnography, but a forensic dissection of the U.S. legal and medical machinery that has policed and punished Black women’s bodies throughout history. Roberts traces not just policy, but the lived consequences for those forced to metabolize the state’s refusal of personhood—a book that finds the wound by following the lives stamped by it.

**(2) Locked In: The True Causes of Mass Incarceration—and How to Achieve Real Reform — John Pfaff**
This is the anatomy of the American criminal justice materialized through numbers, but always returning to the bodies those numbers contain. Pfaff carries the forensic spirit: not accepting the obvious culprits, but tracking the lines of institutional force through the overlooked places—county prosecutors, bail routines, “technical” parole violations—and exposing the hydra-like ways the wound renews itself.

**(3) Life and Death in the Warehouses — Jane Hardy and Mark Harvey (eds)**
A forensic ethnography of precarious labor inside the Amazon and Tesco supply chains in the UK, following how bodies are ground down by algorithmic violence, “productivity” quotas, and the amnesia of the wider public. The book is a map of survival tactics, hidden resistance, and psychic suffering as living immune responses to capital’s refusal to mourn the casualties it produces.

**(4) The Violence of Care: Mexico’s Drug War and the Security of Everyday Life — Cecelia Menjívar, María Elena Ramírez Alarcón**
Traces the trauma and violence that shape life in northern Mexico, not just through the spectacular numbers, but by inhabiting the rituals, code-switching, and micropolitics of streets and homes where security is something performed, never assumed. It is forensic in its devotion to the wound as field condition, not exception.

**(5) No Human Involved: The Story of the Women Who Died in Jail — NHI Collective, edited by Sarah Schulman**
Documents the lives and deaths of marginalized women in Arizona’s jails, focusing on the “invisible” deaths the state does not mourn. The text is itself a forensic ritual—reclaiming memory, restoring dignity to the ritually unmourned—and modeling the structural feedback loop between law, death, and refusal.

**(6) In Search of Respect: Selling Crack in El Barrio — Philippe Bourgois**
Classic ethnography with forensic reach—deep inside Harlem’s crack markets, mapping how families and individuals metabolize the wound of exclusion. Bourgois does not merely study a “market” but follows the logic of how the state’s refusals are re-inscribed in the street’s violence and ethics.

**(7) Ordinary Injustice: How America Holds Court — Amy Bach**
Follows broken courtrooms in places like upstate New York and Mississippi, tracing specific cases in which official procedure efficiently butchers the possibility of justice. Bach’s eye is trained on system-level blindness: not prosecutorial malice, but institutional numbness—a key forensic move.

**(8) The Unspoken as Heritage: The Armenian Genocide and its Unaccounted Lives — Hrag Papazian**
A study of inherited trauma among Armenians in Turkey, mapping un-narrated pain, silences, and the ritual performances that pass the wound generationally. The forensic move here is to track pain by its residues—absences, amnesia, ritualized stories—where open narration is still forbidden.

**(9) Sidewalk — Mitchell Duneier**
A forensic ethnography of street vendors and homeless men in Greenwich Village, New York. Duneier shows how public policy and informal regulation create loops of exclusion and improvisational care—even within one city block.

**(10) Rights of the Dead — Elizabeth Povinelli**
Short but scalpel-sharp, this ethnography follows Indigenous Australians’ everyday rituals for the dead, and the state’s refusal to see their losses as losses. The forensic method here is in following not just what is permitted, but where dignity is absented as a structural form.

All these works share the forensic spirit: they treat suffering as evidence, track harm across systems and bodies, and ask not just how the wound happened, but how it is continually performed and refused by living entities. They are not instruction manuals, nor are they sentimental; they are manuals for tracing the feedback loop between injury, ritual, and the wider body’s immune logic. Their relevance for your paradigm is in how they make wound and refusal visible as a system-level map, not as isolated failure.
