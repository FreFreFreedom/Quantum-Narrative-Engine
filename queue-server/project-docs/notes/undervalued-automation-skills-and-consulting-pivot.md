## The eight phases

Each phase was described by what it is, what gets automated, the main tools, and where Antoine stands.

**1. Niche and offer.** Who you serve, which painful problem you fix, and the price. Nothing to automate yet; "it's the phase most people skip and regret." The reply pointed to his mix of accounting, small technical companies, agriculture and greenhouses, and bilingual French and English. If he is in Quebec, bilingualism is a real advantage with francophone small businesses. Three directions were offered: back-office automation (invoicing, bookkeeping, reporting) for small technical or agricultural companies; automation for accounting firms; operations automation for greenhouse and agtech companies. "You don't need to choose today. You need to choose before phase 2."

**2. Lead generation.** Content, cold email, referrals, networks. Automate finding, enriching, scoring leads and drafting first messages for review. Tools: Apollo or Clay, Instantly, Airtable, Claude. The reply noted his first leads are probably warm: his brother's suppliers, clients, other greenhouses, the accountants he works with. "In a narrow niche, one introduction can beat a thousand cold emails."

**3. Sales.** A discovery call where you "ask questions until the real pain shows," often a paid audit, then a proposal. Automate booking, reminders, transcription, a summary of pains, and a proposal draft from the transcript. Tools: Cal.com or Calendly, Fathom or Fireflies, Claude. The reply called this his biggest gap and biggest growth area: "A technical person who can also run a calm discovery call is rare." *The Mom Test* was named for this phase.

**4. Contract and payment.** Signature, invoice, payment. Almost all of it can be automated: accepted proposal triggers the contract, signing triggers the invoice, payment triggers onboarding. Tools: PandaDoc or Docusign, Stripe, QuickBooks or Xero. "You know accounting, so this phase will feel easy," and many small businesses still chase invoices by hand, which makes it sellable.

**5. Onboarding**, "the phase from the ad." The first days after a yes: welcome email, intake form, project folder, channel, list of access requests, kickoff booking. Tools: n8n or Make as conductor, Tally, Drive or Notion, Airtable. The reply called it a good first build for his own business: "it shows you how a trigger becomes a chain of actions," and the pattern is reused for every client.

**6. Delivery.** Mapping the client's processes, finding the two or three with the best payoff, then building, testing, documenting and handing over. Parts of the mapping can be automated, such as interview transcripts turned into process maps. Tools: n8n or Make, Claude or OpenAI APIs, Claude Code for custom work. Vapi or Retell were mentioned for voice agents, "AI that answers phone calls." The reply said this is his strongest phase and that the Maker School page now lists Claude Code next to n8n. It also raised data privacy: if he touches clients' financial or customer data, the rules are Law 25 in Quebec and PIPEDA federally. "Knowing this will make you more credible with serious clients, especially in accounting."

**7. Retainer.** A monthly fee for monitoring, fixes and new automations: health checks, error alerts, a monthly report of hours saved. "This is where stable income comes from," and it suits his temperament, "the opposite of the all-in sprint."

**8. Proof loop.** Case studies, testimonials and referrals feeding phase 2. Automate a testimonial request 30 days after delivery, a case-study draft from project notes, a referral ask. "Your first case study already exists," the work at his brother's company, written up as before, now, and hours saved per week.

## Pricing ladder

Three rungs: a paid diagnostic or audit (a few hours mapping processes, delivered as a report with priorities), a build project (the top one to three automations at a fixed price), then a retainer. The reply's rule was "Price based on value, not hours." Its example: an automation saving a company 10 hours a week of a $35-an-hour employee is roughly $18,000 a year, so a few thousand dollars to build it is reasonable for them. The reply stressed it was not promising these numbers early. First projects may be cheaper because they buy proof, but "this is the logic to learn," and it is the logic that moves him away from $20 an hour.

## How phases connect: the pricing ladder

A common structure in this field looks like this:

1. **Paid diagnostic/audit:** a few hours mapping their processes, delivered as a report with priorities. It's low risk for them and good practice for you.
2. **Build project:** you implement the top 1–3 automations for a fixed price.
3. **Retainer:** monthly upkeep and new improvements.

Price based on value, not hours. If an automation saves a company 10 hours a week of a $35/hour employee, that's roughly $18,000 a year. Charging a few thousand dollars to build it is reasonable for them. That's the logic that moves you away from $20 an hour. I'm not promising those numbers early on. Your first projects may be cheaper, and that's fine, because they're buying proof. But this is the logic to learn.
