Before you can wage war, you have to define what the world is and who counts as a valid entity within it. The *ontology of war* is the underlying architecture of categories, rules, and relationships that makes a military conflict possible in the first place.

Every civilization builds an ontology of war that reflects its own structure:
* **The Feudal Ontology:** War was a matter of lineage, divine right, and localized territorial control. The basic nodes in the system were noble houses, knights, and fiefdoms. 
* **The Industrial/Nation-State Ontology:** War became a math problem of mass production, sovereign borders, and total mobilization. The nodes were nation-states, standing armies, factory outputs, and railway logistics lines. 
* **The Digital/Algorithmic Ontology:** Today, war is treated as a continuous, global data-flow problem. The nodes are no longer just divisions on a map; they are IP addresses, satellite telemetry feeds, supply chain logistics, cell-phone pings, and algorithmic threat scores.

The *ontology of war* dictates what gets seen and what gets ignored. For example, if a military's ontology defines civilians strictly as "non-combatants outside the targeting matrix," that is a boundary. But when an intelligence apparatus uses a system like Palantir to ingest massive streams of metadata, the boundary shifts. The ontology expands to classify *everything* as a potential data point, a potential node, or a potential threat. 

In short, the ontology of war is the conceptual operating system that decides how a society perceives violence, justifies it, and structures its forces to execute it.
