### Biopolitics in Speculative Fiction

Antoine turned the inquiry toward genre fiction, asking whether science fiction explicitly addresses biopolitics.

The discussion framed speculative fiction not as an escape from biopolitics, but as an explicit stress-test of it: once the genre chrome of starships and neon is stripped away, sci-fi functions primarily as an examination of how governments, corporations, and technologies manage, discipline, and commodify biological bodies. The body ceases to be sacred and is rendered as infrastructure.

The genre's handling of biopolitics was mapped across several variations:
* **Genetic predetermination:** In *Gattaca* (alongside references to *Blade Runner*, *K-PAX*, and *Alita: Battle Angel*), Foucault’s biopower is realized as an exact administrative science, where an individual's genetic code operates as a biopolitical markup that dictates social caste, labor, and life prospects before birth.
* **The commodification of flesh:** In cyberpunk works like *Neuromancer* and *Ghost in the Shell*, the boundary between biology and capital collapses. Organs are harvested, consciousness is digitized, and biological survival is tethered to corporate subscription models, such as proprietary firmware patches for artificial hearts. The state and corporation fuse into a machine deciding who receives optimization and who remains in baseline physical decay.
* **Biopolitical panic and state triage:** In pandemic and reproductive crises like *Children of Men* and *The Last of Us*, biological failure or contagion causes the state to discard democratic procedures in favor of raw biological governance: quarantine camps, militarized borders, and triage ethics.

Sci-fi was thus identified as a scaling-up of the emergency room's underlying logic: taking the invisible triage, algorithmic surveillance, and economic rationing of the modern clinic and expanding them across entire planetary landscapes.

### The Rural Reality: Alexander’s *The Hospital*

Antoine then prompted an exploration of the biopolitics of a specific text: Brian Alexander’s *The Hospital: Life, Death, and Dollars in a Small American Town*.

Taking up Alexander’s account shifted the inquiry from speculative fiction to a documentary autopsy of the same systemic machinery, grounded in the concrete geography of Hicksville, Ohio. In this rural setting, the abstract mechanics of biopower appear without futuristic aestheticization:

* **The Financialization of Flesh:** The antagonist is identified as the economic architecture of American healthcare, where balance sheets, corporate consolidation, and reimbursement formulas govern bodily life. Subsumed into corporate healthcare networks, the small-town hospital shifts its purpose from alleviating suffering to managing throughput, margin, and liabilities. The human body becomes a data point on an actuarial table; when a community lacks the capital to sustain an institution's financial metabolism, the protective shell of care withdraws, abandoning the population to chronic decay and premature mortality.
* **The Micro-Scale as Macro-Mirror:** The rural hospital mirrors the broader decline of the civic fabric. The structural collapse of the surrounding town—deindustrialization, disappearing work, and opioid dependency—washes directly into the emergency room. Frontline nurses and physicians serve as human shock absorbers, attempting to counter structural abandonment through pure personal endurance, while administrative overhead and algorithmic triage enforce market logic that treats human biology as an inconvenient overhead cost.

Alexander’s text demonstrated that the systemic crisis cannot be solved by technical adjustments to insurance policy, because the crisis is structural: the governing civilization prioritizes capital accumulation over biological preservation. The inquiry reached the conclusion that modern medical reality operates on a quiet, institutional axiom: the ledger always outranks the pulse.
